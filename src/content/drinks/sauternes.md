Sauternes is born from rot. Without a microscopic fungus called *Botrytis cinerea*,
the white grapes of southern Bordeaux would yield unremarkable, ordinary dry wine.

In autumn, the cold Ciron stream flows into the warm Garonne River, blanketing the
vineyards in damp, ghostly morning fogs. The fungus blooms on grape skins, puncturing
tiny holes in the flesh. When the midday sun burns off the fog, water evaporates through
the punctures, concentrating sugars, acids, and minerals into shriveled, raisined berries.

The botrytis does not simply concentrate juice; it rewrites the chemistry. It introduces
haunting aromatics found nowhere else: orange blossom honey, beeswax, candied apricot,
saffron, marmalade, and roasted hazelnut.

Viscous and golden in the glass, with vibrant acidity that cuts the intense sweetness,
it is the historic partner for salty Roquefort blue cheese or dark fruit desserts.
