Islay Scotch is the most polarizing spirit in the world. To the uninitiated, it smells
like a burning hospital, road asphalt, or seaweed decaying on a storm-swept beach.
To those who acquire the taste, it is the pinnacle of terroir in distilled spirits.

The smoke is not wood smoke; it is peat. Islay has few trees; for centuries, maltsters
kilned their germinating barley over compressed, waterlogged bog moss rich in sphagnum,
heather roots, and decomposed sea kelp. When burned at low heat, it releases an explosion
of heavy phenolic compounds that bind permanently to the barley starch.

Poured neat into a tulip-shaped Glencairn glass, it reveals surprising layers beneath the
smoke: toasted cereal, sea salt, green apple, vanilla, and sweet peat oil. A few drops of
still water break surface tension, unleashing a tempest of brine and maritime fog.
